Nov 2, 2026 · @B. Henry

Removing a degree requirement costs an employer almost nothing.

Hiring someone without the degree forces the employer to decide how else it will recognize ability.

That is where commitment gets tested.

In February 2024, the Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School's Project on Managing the Future of Work published [*Skills-Based Hiring: The Long Road from Pronouncements to Practice*](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6197797102be715f55c0e0a1/t/65cc355c4935cb001349a4cd/1707881822922/Skills-Based%2BHiring%2B02122024%2BvF.pdf). Across 11,300 roles at large employers that dropped degree requirements, the share of new hires without a bachelor's degree increased by about 3.5 percentage points. But relatively few roles dropped the requirement. Extrapolating nationally, the authors estimated roughly 97,000 additional non-degreed hires in 2023—fewer than 1 in 700 hires across the U.S. economy.

Most firms that dropped the requirement did not sustain a change. About 45% changed in name only: they rewrote the posting and showed no meaningful difference in who they hired. About one-fifth were backsliders. They initially hired more workers without degrees, but the gains did not last.

A press release can't settle this. One question can: who got the job?

A company can remove "bachelor's degree required" from a posting and still prefer applicants whose histories look familiar. The degree leaves the ad and keeps doing its work in the interview room.

Experience requirements work the same way. Five years at a recognizable company is easy to read. Evidence that someone can do the work takes effort to assess.

There are good reasons to value education and relevant experience, and some jobs require particular qualifications. An employer that claims to hire for skills still has to name the skills and explain how candidates demonstrate them.

"Strong communication" is not an assessment method. Neither is "executive presence."

If a role requires explaining technical problems to customers, give candidates a representative problem and ask them to explain it. Decide beforehand what a good answer must accomplish. Hold every candidate to that standard.

Keep the exercise short and use fictional material. If it takes hours, pay for them. Applicants should not have to provide free consulting to prove they deserve a conversation.

An assessment will not eliminate bias. It does give the hiring team something to discuss besides whether a résumé feels reassuring.

The same report points to what employers may give up by stopping at the posting. At the firms that actually changed who they hired, workers without degrees had a two-year retention rate 10 percentage points higher than their degreed colleagues. That is a pattern among firms already doing the work, not a guarantee. It is still more than a rewritten posting can claim.

So count the result. After the requirements change, look at who was hired. Then follow those people and see how they performed and whether they got the support to succeed.

A company that tracks those outcomes can learn whether its policy works. A company that counts rewritten postings can announce progress without knowing whether anyone benefited.

This matters most when an employer says qualified people are impossible to find. The shortage may be real. The company should still inspect the process that decided everyone else was unqualified.

Before celebrating the next skills-first announcement, ask who got through the door.

The job ad is a promise. The hiring decision is the evidence.
