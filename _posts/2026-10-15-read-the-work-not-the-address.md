An address tells you where an applicant lives. Somewhere between the résumé and the shortlist, it can become an assessment of what they can do.

That transition deserves a second look.

Mississippi has a small tech sector: 1,880 software developers statewide in May 2025. That describes the market, not the person applying from it. Once you have their work in front of you, the state's reputation should have less to contribute. A work sample answers a question the address never could.

An unfamiliar employer presents the same problem. If you recognize the company, you may already have some idea of its technical demands. If you don't, you have something to look up.

Stennis Space Center is NASA's premier rocket propulsion testing facility, and it tested the SLS core stage for Artemis I. The University of Mississippi Medical Center reported more than $100 million in research awards in fiscal 2025. Mississippi State and the University of Mississippi hold the top Carnegie research classification, R1. If those names tell you little, investigate what the applicant did there. Unfamiliarity is a gap in the evaluator's knowledge. It should not become a mark against the applicant.

Salary history needs similar care. The median Mississippi software developer earned $95,330 in May 2025, about 30 percent below the national median of $135,980. Local compensation reflects a local labor market. Carry that number into a different market as a measure of ability, and the original pay gap becomes a hiring judgment. Nothing about the person's work changed.

The tempting response is to assemble a Mississippi honor roll: successful founders, major research institutions, people who built things nobody expected them to build from here. Those examples can challenge an assumption. They cannot establish the ability of the next applicant.

Fortunately, they don't have to.

An applicant should not need a famous Mississippian to make their own work worth examining. You are not hiring the state or estimating its average. You are deciding whether one person can do a particular job.

Location can answer legitimate questions. Can the applicant work the required hours? Does the role require an office or relocation? Ask those questions directly. A logistical requirement belongs in the decision under its own name, rather than folded into a vague concern about fit.

Then read the application again with the address hidden. Examine a sample. Ask the applicant to explain a decision they made on something they shipped, and what happened afterward.

If revealing the address changes your assessment, identify what information it added. A time-zone conflict is an answer. An office requirement is an answer. A feeling about Mississippi needs evidence.

The applicant has supplied work you can inspect.

Start there.
