# You Can't Keep Demanding Experience While Cutting the Jobs That Produce It

A company decides it no longer needs a junior analyst. AI can produce the reports.

Two years later, the same company wants to hire someone who can tell when those reports are wrong.

Where does it expect that person to have learned?

Somewhere else, presumably. At another company, on somebody else's budget.

That is an appealing hiring strategy until enough employers adopt it.

## Experience has to happen somewhere

Imagine a new analyst working through a spreadsheet. Two totals disagree. She asks a colleague why. One includes customers who have left; the other does not.

The report goes to a manager. The analyst remembers to check the definitions next time.

An AI system might produce the correct report immediately. It might also produce a convincing report using the wrong total. An experienced analyst knows where to look because she has encountered that kind of mistake before.

The employer needs the report today. It will eventually need someone who understands the customers behind the numbers. Automating the report does not answer how that person gets trained.

There is no reason to preserve every tedious assignment. Some work teaches very little. But employers should know which assignments develop useful judgment before removing them, and what will provide that experience afterward.

"You can review what the AI produces" is an answer only if someone teaches the new hire how.

## The door is closing before people get through it

The August revision of Stanford's "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" examined ADP payroll data through June 2026. Employment among workers ages 22 to 25 in the two most AI-exposed fifths of occupations fell about 11% from November 2022. In the three least-exposed fifths, it grew about 10%. The divergence primarily reflected reduced hiring, rather than increased departures. ([Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf))

These are descriptive findings, not proof that AI caused the gap. Education controls substantially reduce the estimates, and the pattern is stronger in the ADP sample than in national survey comparisons. The authors also report differences between occupations that began before ChatGPT.

Those limits matter. So does the hiring result. A company can retain its experienced staff while quietly reducing the opportunities through which people become experienced.

The consequences will not all appear in a layoff announcement. Some will appear in jobs that never open.

## Somebody else's training budget

Luis Garicano has already named the economic problem. In "The AI Becker Problem," he explains how AI can undermine the work that helps juniors finance their training. Garicano and Luis Rayo formalize that argument in "Training in the Age of AI." Their model also allows AI to increase the value of expertise enough to sustain the arrangement. Collapse is a possibility under specified conditions, not an inevitable outcome. ([siliconcontinent.com](https://www.siliconcontinent.com/p/the-ai-becker-problem), [cepr.org](https://cepr.org/publications/dp20634))

The distinction between paying for training and learning from a task is important. Routine work can help finance instruction even when the task itself teaches little. Other assignments offer both useful output and a chance to learn.

Becker's original point still applies. A firm that trains someone in general skills can lose her to a competitor that paid nothing. Every employer has a reason to let another one carry that cost. Telling managers they ought to carry it anyway does not change that reason.

Garicano and Rayo's condition gives managers a better question. In their model, training stays at least as profitable as it was before AI when a fully trained expert is worth enough more than what AI produces alone. That turns an obligation into a business test. In your domain, does AI make your experienced people much more valuable?

If it does, the training can still pay for itself. A junior has reason to accept lower starting pay in exchange for learning from people whose expertise is worth that much. That is how Becker expected general training to be financed. The firm that cuts the role gives up that arrangement and has to hire the expertise later. If enough other firms made the same cut, the person it wants will be scarce and priced accordingly.

If it does not, the honest conclusion is different. Individual firms will not fund the training, and the remedy has to be collective. Garicano says as much: either public policy changes the incentives, or some other way of paying for training has to emerge.

The model is a theory. It cannot tell a particular manager which side of the line her team is on. It does tell her what to estimate before cutting the role.

Demanding experienced applicants is easy. Producing them costs money.

## Review is a skill, too

Handing a new employee an AI-generated report and making them responsible for its accuracy is a particularly convenient way to avoid this expense.

The employee has a deliverable. The manager has a human reviewer. On paper, the process has oversight.

But what is the reviewer supposed to recognize?

If she has never reconciled conflicting definitions, she may not know that two reasonable-looking totals answer different questions. Asking her to approve the report does not supply that knowledge. It gives her responsibility for a decision she has not been prepared to make.

An employer that wants useful review has to teach it. That requires access to the source material and someone willing to explain why a correction was necessary.

AI may make those conversations easier. It cannot put them on a manager's calendar.

## Give the junior something to learn

The Stanford paper offers a clue about which roles to test. To separate automation from assistance, the authors use the Anthropic Economic Index, which has Claude classify its own conversations. A directive request, where the user hands over a task, counts as automation. Learning and validation count as augmentation. Entry-level declines concentrate in occupations where AI use is mostly automation. Where use is mostly complementary, young workers show no comparable decline.

Treat that carefully. A model labeling its own conversations is an imperfect measure, and the results are aggregated by occupation. No firm's staffing decision is observed. The finding does not show that redesigning a role works. It shows which kind of role to try.

A junior analyst could compare the model's report with its source data and discuss discrepancies with a senior colleague. Over time, the colleague should be able to step back because the analyst recognizes more of the problems herself.

That is a job design worth testing, not one the Stanford study proved. The employer has to find out.

After six months, give the analyst a fresh problem. Can she explain which definition applies? Can she defend the number she chose when the model offers a different one?

If she can, the team has developed someone. If she cannot, finishing more reports has concealed a training problem.

This takes senior time. A manager who removes that time should acknowledge the tradeoff. The employee's failure to develop will not be evidence that she lacked initiative if nobody gave her a reasonable opportunity to learn.

Employers can redesign junior work around AI. They can decide some tasks no longer justify a position. What they cannot do is count on a supply of experienced people that their own hiring helped shrink.

Before cutting the next entry-level job, answer the question you will eventually put to a recruiter.
